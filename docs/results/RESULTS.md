# Results: does the composition of a pretraining corpus matter, and how much of it do you need?

Scored 28 November 2026, after the overnight campaign finished. Every number
here comes from `docs/results/report_numbers.md`, which is regenerated by
`scripts/plot_results_report.py` from the raw per-cell predictions. Nothing in
this document is typed by hand.

---

## How to read anything below

Three benchmarks, each with its own molecules, its own frozen splits and its own
metric:

| Benchmark | What it asks | Molecules | Metric | Label budgets |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Biological origin** | is this natural product of plant, fungal or bacterial origin? | 60,170 | AUROC, higher better | 100 / 500 / 1,000 / 5,000 |
| **Vapour pressure** | how volatile is this molecule? | OPERA / PHYSPROP, ~2,400 | RMSE log10 mmHg, lower better | 100 / 500 / 1,000 / all (~1,894) |
| **PTP1B inhibition** | how strongly does this natural product inhibit PTP1B? | NPASS, ~460 | RMSE potency, lower better | 50 / 100 / all (~365) |

Rules that hold everywhere in this document and in every figure:

- **Everything is paired on the split.** Two encoders are always compared on the
  same molecules, the same frozen split and the same label budget. There are 10
  splits per benchmark, and the split is the unit of replication. Intervals are
  95% t-intervals on the 10 split-level differences.
- **Several pretraining draws of one condition are averaged inside a split
  first.** Two draws of PubChem-25k are two draws of one condition, not two extra
  replicates.
- **Differences are oriented so that bigger is better.** RMSE differences are
  sign-flipped. "+0.07" always means better, on every benchmark.
- **The three origin tasks are averaged inside a split** (they share molecules
  and splits). Vapour pressure and PTP1B share nothing with each other or with
  origin and are never pooled.

Two reference points matter more than any other:

- **No pretraining** — the identical network trained from scratch. Beating it
  says pretraining did something.
- **Chemprop + 1,613 Mordred descriptors** — the decisive one. CheMeleon is
  pretrained *by regressing those exact descriptors*. So an encoder that beats a
  fingerprint but not a model simply handed the descriptors has shown a
  descriptor effect, not a corpus effect. **This is the bar.**

---

## The short version

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Does pretraining help at all? | **Yes on all three benchmarks**, but only above a label budget floor. |
| Does it beat just handing the model the descriptors? | **Only on biological origin.** On vapour pressure it loses. On PTP1B it ties. |
| Does the *composition* of the corpus matter? | **No effect detectable anywhere.** Natural products, NP-like ZINC, general ZINC and general PubChem all land on top of each other. |
| How much pretraining data do you need? | **About 5,000–25,000 molecules.** Past 25k the curve is flat; 944k is no better than 25k. |
| Is anything not interpretable? | **Yes — vapour pressure at 100 labels.** It fails its own noise check. |

## Goal 1 — Does pretraining help?

**Figure: `docs/figures/report_headline.png`**

Measured against the same network with no pretraining, at each benchmark's
largest budget:

| Benchmark | Best pretrained encoder | Gain over no pretraining |
|---|---|---|
| Biological origin, 5,000 labels | ZINC general 25k | **+1.40 ± 0.56 AUROC points** |
| Vapour pressure, all labels | PubChem 100k | **+0.116 ± 0.089 RMSE** |
| PTP1B, all labels | PubChem 100k | **+0.074 ± 0.046 RMSE** |

Pretraining works on all three. But it needs labels to show up:

- On **biological origin**, nothing is distinguishable at 100 labels — every
  interval is wide enough to swallow the effect. From 500 labels upward the gain
  is solid and grows no further.
- On **PTP1B**, the gain is present already at 50 labels (+0.08 ± 0.05) and
  essentially identical at every budget.
- On **vapour pressure**, the gain is the weakest of the three and only
  separates from zero at 1,000 labels and above.

One oddity worth recording: on biological origin at 100 and 500 labels, plain
**Chemprop with no descriptors is clearly *worse* than no pretraining at all**
(−5.75 ± 3.40 at 100 labels). A pretrained encoder started badly can be worse
than a fresh one when labels are scarce.

## Goal 2 — Does it beat the descriptors? (The bar that matters)

**Figure: `docs/figures/benchmark_panel.png`**

### Biological origin: yes, clearly

At 500 labels and above, every pretrained encoder beats Chemprop+descriptors,
and the gap is stable:

| Encoder | 500 labels | 1,000 labels | 5,000 labels |
|---|---|---|---|
| PubChem 25k | +3.06 ± 1.06 ✓ | +1.76 ± 0.87 ✓ | +1.86 ± 0.63 ✓ |
| COCONUT 25k, benchmark-free | +3.09 ± 1.00 ✓ | +1.53 ± 1.02 ✓ | +1.98 ± 0.52 ✓ |
| ZINC NP-like 25k | +2.33 ± 0.86 ✓ | +1.68 ± 0.99 ✓ | +1.86 ± 0.58 ✓ |
| ZINC general 25k | +2.12 ± 0.99 ✓ | +1.70 ± 0.78 ✓ | +2.02 ± 0.64 ✓ |
| Released CheMeleon 944k | +2.25 ± 1.71 ✓ | +1.64 ± 0.65 ✓ | +1.87 ± 0.50 ✓ |

**About two AUROC points, consistently, from every corpus.** This is the one
place in the study where pretraining is worth doing over simply computing the
descriptors.

### Vapour pressure: no — the descriptors win outright

**Not one encoder beats Chemprop+descriptors at any budget.** Most are
significantly *worse*:

| Encoder | 100 labels | 500 labels | 1,000 labels | all labels |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PubChem 25k | −0.768 ± 0.293 ✗ | −0.215 ± 0.155 ✗ | −0.144 ± 0.143 ✗ | −0.072 ± 0.125 |
| COCONUT 25k, benchmark-free | −0.832 ± 0.278 ✗ | −0.281 ± 0.152 ✗ | −0.212 ± 0.146 ✗ | −0.160 ± 0.145 ✗ |
| PubChem 100k | −0.507 ± 0.222 ✗ | −0.212 ± 0.175 ✗ | −0.107 ± 0.170 | −0.040 ± 0.143 |
| Released CheMeleon 944k | −0.278 ± 0.272 ✗ | +0.018 ± 0.115 | +0.031 ± 0.134 | −0.056 ± 0.140 |

The released 944k model is the only one that manages a tie. On this benchmark
the descriptor baseline is simply the strongest thing in the campaign.

### PTP1B: a tie

No encoder clears the bar, but none falls below it either. The best is the
released model at 50 labels, +0.048 ± 0.059 — the interval straddles zero. At
full labels the 100k model reaches +0.044 ± 0.057. Pretraining matches the
descriptors here but does not improve on them.

## Goal 3 — Does the composition of the corpus matter?

**Figure: `docs/figures/report_composition.png`**

This is the question the project exists to answer. Four corpora, all at 25,000
molecules, all compared against a general PubChem draw of the same size, so only
*what is in the corpus* differs:

- PubChem with benchmark molecules removed
- COCONUT natural products, benchmark-free
- ZINC filtered to be natural-product-like
- ZINC general

**No corpus separates from PubChem anywhere.** Across all three benchmarks and
all eleven task-by-budget blocks, every interval overlaps the noise floor — the
band showing how far apart two *random draws of PubChem itself* land.

The clearest block, biological origin at 5,000 labels, where intervals are
tightest:

| Corpus | vs PubChem 25k | Noise floor |
|---|---|---|
| PubChem, benchmark-free | +0.08 ± 0.29 | ±0.65 |
| COCONUT, benchmark-free | +0.12 ± 0.23 | ±0.65 |
| ZINC NP-like | −0.00 ± 0.20 | ±0.65 |
| ZINC general | +0.16 ± 0.24 | ±0.65 |

All four sit within a quarter of an AUROC point of PubChem, with intervals far
narrower than the two-point gain pretraining itself buys. **Whatever pretraining
is teaching the network, it is not something a natural-product corpus teaches
better than a general one.**

Note the direction on PTP1B in particular: PTP1B is a *natural product*
benchmark, built from NPASS. If a natural-product corpus were going to pay off
anywhere, it would be here. It does not — COCONUT lands at +0.001 ± 0.013
against PubChem at full labels.

**One caveat that stays open.** The arm that would have separated "natural
products help" from "chemistry resembling the test set helps"
(`pubchem_nearbench`) was cancelled from this campaign on 27 November to stay
inside the GPU quota. That alternative *is* tested separately on ESOL in
Amendment 18, where it comes out flat at both budgets (+0.0014 [−0.061, +0.064]
at full labels), so proximity to the benchmark buys nothing there. That does not
transfer automatically to these two benchmarks.

## Goal 4 — How much pretraining data do you need?

**Figure: `docs/figures/report_size.png`**

PubChem pretrained at seven sizes from 500 to 100,000 molecules, two draws each,
plus the released 944k model.

On **biological origin**, measured against no pretraining:

| Pretraining molecules | 500 labels | 5,000 labels |
|---|---|---|
| 500 | +0.72 ± 1.99 | +0.23 ± 0.66 |
| 1,000 | +1.14 ± 1.43 | +0.60 ± 0.61 |
| 5,000 | +2.69 ± 1.43 ✓ | +0.76 ± 0.42 ✓ |
| 25,000 | +3.42 ± 1.56 ✓ | +1.24 ± 0.49 ✓ |
| 100,000 | +2.66 ± 1.65 ✓ | +1.37 ± 0.67 ✓ |
| 944,000 (released) | +2.61 ± 1.99 ✓ | +1.25 ± 0.73 ✓ |

**The curve rises steeply to about 5,000 molecules, reaches its value by 25,000,
and is flat after that.** Going from 25,000 to 944,000 — a 38-fold increase —
buys nothing measurable on any of the three benchmarks. On PTP1B the same shape
holds: 500 molecules gives +0.002, 5,000 gives +0.056, and 25,000 through 944k
all sit around +0.07.

This is the most practically useful result in the study: **a few tens of
thousands of molecules is enough.**

## What is not interpretable, and why

**Figure: `docs/figures/report_noise_floor.png`**

Before reading any corpus comparison, the design has to pass its own check. Take
two draws of the *same* corpus at the *same* size — PubChem 25k draw 0 against
draw 1. Nothing but chance separates them, so the interval must contain zero. Its
half-width is the smallest effect this design can distinguish from a redraw.

| Benchmark | Budget | Floor mean | Half-width | Passes? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Biological origin | 100 | −1.006 | 1.478 | yes |
| Biological origin | 500 | +0.142 | 0.599 | yes |
| Biological origin | 1,000 | +0.038 | 0.918 | yes |
| Biological origin | 5,000 | −0.395 | 0.649 | yes |
| **Vapour pressure** | **100** | **+0.147** | **0.121** | **NO** |
| Vapour pressure | 500 | +0.012 | 0.081 | yes |
| Vapour pressure | 1,000 | +0.034 | 0.068 | yes |
| Vapour pressure | all | +0.042 | 0.047 | yes |
| PTP1B | 50 | +0.004 | 0.011 | yes |
| PTP1B | 100 | +0.012 | 0.024 | yes |
| PTP1B | all | −0.005 | 0.016 | yes |

**Vapour pressure at 100 labels fails.** Two draws of one corpus differ by more
than their interval allows. Since only the random draw of pretraining molecules
separates those encoders, that gap cannot be a corpus effect — it means that
cell is noisy enough to manufacture a significant-looking difference out of
nothing. **Nothing at that budget should be read, in either direction**,
including the negative results reported above.

The likely cause is already on record: acyclic molecules all share the empty
Murcko scaffold, so the vapour pressure splits are lumpier than intended, and
that bites hardest where the training set is smallest. Fixing those splits is
the one piece of follow-up work this result actively motivates.

The other ten blocks pass, with floors well below the contrasts being drawn. The
nulls at those budgets are real nulls, not failures to measure.

## Overall assessment against the project's question

> *Does the composition of a molecular pretraining corpus matter, and how much of
> it do you need?*

**Composition: no measurable effect.** Across three benchmarks, eleven
task-by-budget blocks and four corpora spanning general chemistry, filtered
natural-product-like chemistry and actual natural products, nothing separates
from a general PubChem draw by more than the corpora's own draw-to-draw noise.
This holds even on PTP1B, a natural-product benchmark where a natural-product
corpus had every opportunity to win.

**Amount: about 5,000–25,000 molecules, and then it stops.** The size ladder
saturates early and a 944,000-molecule corpus performs like a 25,000-molecule
one.

**A finding neither question anticipated:** pretraining only beats the
descriptor baseline on one of the three benchmarks. On vapour pressure, simply
handing Chemprop the 1,613 Mordred descriptors beats every pretrained encoder in
the study. Since CheMeleon is pretrained to predict those very descriptors, the
honest reading is that on that benchmark pretraining is a lossy route to
information that is available directly — and that any corpus study has to clear
this baseline before a corpus effect can be claimed at all.
